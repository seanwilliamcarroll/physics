Boids: three rules, a flock, and what finding neighbours costs

Each boid sees only the boids within its visual range: it steers away from any inside its protected range (separation), toward the rest's average heading (alignment) and their centre (cohesion), turns back near the edges, and keeps its speed in a band (Reynolds 1987, in the ECE 4760 course's form and tuning). Colour is heading, so a group flying together is one colour. Move the pointer over the flock: it's a predator, and they flee it. Turn matching to 0 and the flock never forms: the polarization below stays near 0. Edge turning is soft, so the flock overshoots the walls as a body (docs/15-swarm.md).

Finding neighbours is the cost. Brute force checks every pair, n² a step; the grid checks the 3 × 3 cells around each boid; sorted also moves boids into cell order every step so those cells are contiguous memory, read by a branchless loop that runs as SIMD instructions. All three find exactly the same neighbours.

Over the whole sky it climbs slowly (10k boids: about 0.5 after a minute) because many separate flocks head different ways; each flock is ordered within seconds, as the colours show. It never settles: flocks merge, split and turn.
